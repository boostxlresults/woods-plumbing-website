When a water heater starts acting up, the first question most homeowners in Marana and Northwest Tucson ask is simple: should I fix it or replace it? The honest answer depends on the age of the unit, what failed, and how hard our local water has been on it. This guide walks through the factors we use on every service call so you can make the decision with confidence.

## Start with the age of the unit

Age is the single biggest factor. You can usually find the manufacture date on the label on the side of the tank, often coded into the serial number. If you are not sure how to read it, a plumber can tell you in a minute.

- **Under 6 years old:** Repair is almost always the right call, unless the tank itself is leaking. Many parts may still be under the manufacturer warranty.
- **6 to 10 years old:** Repair often makes sense for a single failed part. If the unit has needed several repairs or is full of sediment, start weighing replacement.
- **10 years or older:** Replacement is usually the better value, especially for a tank-style heater. Southern Arizona's hard water tends to shorten tank life compared with areas that have softer water.

## Why hard water matters so much here

Tucson-area water is hard, which means it carries a lot of dissolved calcium and magnesium. When water is heated, those minerals settle out and collect as sediment in the bottom of the tank. Over time that sediment:

- Insulates the water from the burner or lower element, so the heater runs longer and costs more to operate.
- Causes popping, rumbling or crackling sounds as water trapped under the sediment boils.
- Overheats the bottom of the tank, which speeds up corrosion and can lead to a leak.
- Takes up space, so you run out of hot water sooner.

The sacrificial anode rod inside the tank also wears out faster in hard water. Once it is gone, the tank itself starts to corrode.

## Problems that are usually worth repairing

These failures typically involve a replaceable part, and fixing them on a healthy tank makes sense:

- **No hot water on an electric heater:** often a failed heating element or thermostat.
- **No hot water on a gas heater:** often a thermocouple, flame sensor, igniter or gas control valve.
- **Water not hot enough:** a thermostat that needs adjustment or replacement, or one failed element on a two-element electric heater.
- **Leaking from a pipe connection or valve:** loose fittings or a failed temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve, which are usually straightforward repairs.
- **Rumbling with no other problems on a younger unit:** a professional flush to remove sediment may restore performance.

## Signs it is time to replace

These usually mean the heater is near the end of its life:

- **Water leaking from the tank itself.** A leak from the body of the tank cannot be repaired. Shut off the water to the heater and plan for replacement.
- **Rusty or discolored hot water** (with clear cold water), which points to corrosion inside the tank.
- **Heavy sediment that will not flush out**, or loud rumbling on an older unit.
- **Repeated repairs.** If you have paid for two or more repairs in the last couple of years, the next dollar is usually better spent on a new unit.
- **Not enough hot water for your household** because your family has grown or the unit was undersized to begin with.

## A simple way to think about the cost

A common rule of thumb: if a repair costs more than about half the price of a new installed heater, and the unit is past the middle of its expected life, replacement is usually the smarter choice. You avoid paying for a repair on a heater that is likely to need another one soon, and a new unit will be more efficient.

Ask your plumber for upfront pricing on both options so you can compare them side by side.

## Tank or tankless?

If you are replacing, it is a good time to consider whether a tankless water heater makes sense for your home.

**Tank water heaters**
- Lower upfront cost and simpler installation when replacing a tank of the same type.
- Store a fixed amount of hot water, so very heavy use can run them out.

**Tankless water heaters**
- Heat water on demand, so they do not run out during back-to-back showers.
- Take up much less space and usually last longer than tank units.
- Cost more upfront and may need gas line, venting or electrical upgrades.
- Still need regular descaling in hard water to keep their heat exchangers clear.

For many households, a correctly sized tank heater is still the most practical choice. For larger homes, families with high hot water use, or homeowners planning to stay long-term, tankless is often worth a look.

## Protect your new (or current) heater

A few habits make a big difference in our water:

- **Flush the tank about once a year** to remove sediment. Homes with especially hard water may benefit from more frequent flushing.
- **Check the anode rod** every few years and replace it when it is worn.
- **Test the T&P relief valve** once a year.
- **Consider a water softener.** Softened water dramatically cuts scale buildup in water heaters, fixtures and appliances.
- **Descale tankless units** on the schedule the manufacturer recommends.

## Water heater safety

Call a licensed plumber right away if you notice:

- A gas smell near a gas water heater. Leave the house and call from outside.
- Scorch marks, soot or a yellow flame at the burner.
- Water pooling around the base of the tank.
- A T&P valve that is discharging water or steam.
