If your water bill jumped for no reason, you hear water running when every faucet is off, or one patch of floor feels warm under your bare feet, you may have a slab leak. Slab leaks are one of the most common serious plumbing problems we see in Marana and Northwest Tucson, and they are also one of the most misunderstood. This guide explains what a slab leak is, why homes in our area get them, how to check for one yourself, how a plumber finds the exact spot, and how to choose between the repair options.

## What is a slab leak?

Most homes in Marana, Oro Valley and Northwest Tucson are built on a concrete slab foundation rather than over a crawl space or basement. The water supply lines that feed your kitchen, bathrooms and water heater often run underneath or inside that slab. A slab leak is any leak in those buried lines.

Because the pipe is under concrete, the water has nowhere obvious to go. It can soak into the soil for weeks or months before anything shows up inside the house. That is why slab leaks tend to be found by their side effects (a high bill, a warm floor, a damp baseboard) instead of by a visible drip.

There are two kinds:

- **Hot water slab leaks** happen on the lines running from the water heater. They are the more common type and often produce a warm or hot spot on the floor.
- **Cold water slab leaks** happen on the main supply or cold branches. They usually show up as a spinning water meter, a damp area, or a rising bill, without any heat.

## Why slab leaks are common in Marana and Northwest Tucson

A few local conditions work against the pipes under your floor:

- **Hard water.** Tucson-area water carries a heavy mineral load. Over the years, minerals and the chemistry of hot water wear on the inside of copper lines, especially hot lines that see constant flow and heat.
- **Pipe age and material.** A large share of homes in Northwest Tucson and older parts of Marana were built from the 1970s through the early 2000s, often with copper lines laid directly in or under the slab. Decades of use catch up with that pipe.
- **Abrasion and movement.** Pipes that touch concrete, gravel or rebar can rub as they expand and contract with temperature changes. Desert soils also shift with moisture, and that small movement puts stress on buried lines.
- **High water pressure.** Pressure that runs higher than the plumbing was designed for speeds up wear everywhere in the system. A failing or missing pressure-reducing valve is a common contributor.

None of this means your home will develop a slab leak. It does mean that if you live in an older slab-built home here, it is worth knowing the signs.

## Warning signs of a slab leak

Watch for any of these, especially two or more together:

1. **An unexplained jump in your water bill** with no change in how your household uses water.
2. **The sound of running water** in a wall or under the floor when every fixture is off.
3. **A warm or hot spot on the floor**, often noticed on tile or by pets choosing an unusual spot to lie down.
4. **Damp or warped flooring**, lifting tile, or a musty smell near baseboards.
5. **Cracks in the floor or walls** that appear or grow without another explanation.
6. **Low water pressure** throughout the home.
7. **A water heater that runs constantly** because it keeps reheating water that is leaking away.
8. **Wet soil or standing water** next to the foundation outside, when it has not rained and irrigation is off.

## How to check for a leak yourself

You can do a simple meter test in about an hour:

1. Turn off every faucet, shower, toilet, ice maker, washing machine and irrigation system. Make sure nobody uses water during the test.
2. Find your water meter (usually in a box near the street) and note the reading. Many meters also have a small triangle or dial called a leak indicator.
3. If the leak indicator is moving with everything off, water is going somewhere.
4. If you want a second check, write down the reading, wait one to two hours without using any water, and read it again. Any change points to a leak.

A moving meter does not prove the leak is under the slab. Running toilets, a dripping irrigation valve, or a leaking hose bibb can do the same thing. Check those first. If they are fine and the meter still moves, call a plumber before you open any flooring.

**Do not start breaking tile to look for the leak.** Water travels along the pipe and through the soil, so the wet spot is often several feet from the actual break.

## How plumbers locate a slab leak

Modern leak detection is about finding the exact spot before anyone touches the floor. A typical visit includes:

- **Isolating the system.** Shutting off and pressure-testing hot and cold sides separately to confirm which line is leaking.
- **Acoustic listening equipment.** Sensitive ground microphones pick up the sound of water escaping under pressure through the slab.
- **Thermal imaging.** For hot water leaks, an infrared camera shows the heat pattern of escaping hot water through flooring.
- **Line tracing.** Locating the actual path of the pipe under the slab so the leak can be pinned to a specific spot on that path.

The goal is to narrow the leak to a small area, which keeps any concrete work as small and inexpensive as possible.

## Your repair options

Once the leak is found, there are three main approaches. The right one depends on the age and condition of the rest of your piping, where the leak is, and what is on the floor above it.

### Spot repair (direct access)

The plumber opens a small section of floor directly above the leak, cuts out the damaged pipe, repairs it, and the slab is patched.

- **Best for:** A single leak in otherwise healthy pipe, under flooring that is easy to replace.
- **Keep in mind:** If the pipe is old and worn, another leak can show up elsewhere on the same line.

### Reroute (bypass the slab)

The leaking line is abandoned in place, and a new line is run through the walls or attic to the same fixtures.

- **Best for:** Leaks under expensive flooring, cabinets or hard-to-reach areas, and homes where the pipe has started to fail in more than one place.
- **Keep in mind:** Requires some drywall access, which is easier and cheaper to repair than concrete and tile.

### Repipe (replace the system)

All or most of the supply piping in the home is replaced, usually with PEX or copper run through walls and ceilings instead of under the slab.

- **Best for:** Homes that have had more than one slab leak, have widespread corrosion, or have older galvanized supply lines.
- **Keep in mind:** It is the largest project of the three, but it takes the water lines out of the slab for good.

A good plumber explains all of the options that fit your situation, with the pros, cons and an upfront price for each, and then lets you choose.

## What about insurance?

Many homeowner policies cover water damage from a sudden leak and the cost of accessing the pipe (tear-out and replacement of flooring), but do not cover the repair of the pipe itself. Coverage varies a great deal from policy to policy. Call your insurer early, take photos, and keep your plumber's written findings. ## When to call right away

Shut off your main water valve and call for emergency service if:

- Water is visibly coming up through the floor or pooling indoors.
- You smell gas, or the water heater area is flooding.
- The ceiling or walls are wet near electrical fixtures.
